HYPOGLYCAEMIA MANAGEMENT — ADULT INPATIENTS (Nursing Protocol NP-ENDO-014, v3.2, effective 1 July 2025)
1. If capillary glucose < 4.0 mmol/L and the patient is conscious and able to swallow: give 15 g fast-acting carbohydrate (150 ml juice or 3 glucose tablets).
2. Recheck CBG in 15 minutes. Repeat step 1 up to 3 times.
3. If unconscious or NBM: IV dextrose 50% 25 ml (or D10% 150 ml) via a large-bore cannula; call the MO on pager 4412.
4. Once CBG > 4.0, give long-acting carbohydrate (2 slices of bread) unless the next meal is within 30 min.
5. Document in the hypoglycaemia chart (form MR-117) and inform the primary team.
Wards 7B, 8A and ICU use the same algorithm; paediatric wards refer to PP-PAED-009.
6. For patients on an insulin infusion, stop the infusion for 15 minutes, treat as above, then restart at half the previous rate once CBG > 6.0 mmol/L.

=== v6_n19 | teaching note ===
Tutorial for Year 4 students — Approach to hyponatraemia
Step 1: confirm true hypotonic hyponatraemia (serum osmolality < 275 mOsm/kg). Exclude pseudohyponatraemia (lipids, paraproteins) and hyperglycaemia (corrected Na = measured Na + 2.4 x (glucose - 5.5)/5.5).
Step 2: clinical assessment of volume status is unreliable, so use urine Na and urine osmolality.
Step 3: urine Na > 30 mmol/L with euvolaemia suggests SIADH, hypothyroidism or glucocorticoid deficiency. Thiazides are a common culprit in the elderly.
Case vignette: a 78-year-old woman on hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg OM presents with Na 118 mmol/L and confusion. Discuss the safe rate of correction (no more than 8-10 mmol/L in 24 h) and the risk of osmotic demyelination.
Discussion points: when to use 3% saline (severe symptoms such as seizures), why fluid restriction fails when urine osmolality is high, and how to monitor sodium every 4-6 hours during correction.
Reference: ward guideline GL-MED-022, updated January 2026.
